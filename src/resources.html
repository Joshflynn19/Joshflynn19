What to Gather Before Requesting a Refrigerant Conversion Quote

Before you contact ANP Marine — or any refrigeration service company — for a refrigerant conversion quotation, gathering the right information from your vessel will save significant time and produce a more accurate quote. Incomplete enquiries result in assumptions, which result in surprises. This guide tells you exactly what to collect.

Why this matters: A refrigerant conversion is not a simple gas swap. The new refrigerant may require different oil, new filter driers, resized expansion valves, updated controls and revised system pressures. The more information you provide upfront, the more precise the quote — and the fewer unexpected costs during the job.

1. Current Refrigerant Information

Refrigerant designationR22, R404A, R407C, R410A, R134a or other. Check the nameplate and the F-Gas logbook.
Total system charge (kg)From the F-Gas equipment log, service records or nameplate. If unknown, note the receiver/cylinder sizes.
GWP and CO₂-equivalentUsed to determine leak check frequency under F-Gas regulations. Your current logbook should show this.
Current oil typeMineral, alkylbenzene (AB) or POE. This determines whether an oil flush is required for the new refrigerant.

2. Compressor Details

Make and modelBITZER, Grasso, Carrier, Copeland, Mycom etc. Photograph the nameplate — do not rely on memory.
Serial numberRequired to identify correct valve plates, gaskets and approved refrigerant alternatives for that specific model.
Year of manufactureOlder compressors may not be approved for certain HFO blends. Year determines available retrofit options.
Running hoursHeavily run compressors may require overhaul before conversion. Include hours since last major service.
Compressor typeReciprocating, screw, scroll or rotary. This affects oil type, conversion approach and expected performance change.
Number of compressors and circuitsHow many independent refrigeration circuits does the system have? Are they stand-alone or connected?

3. System Components

Expansion valve type and modelTEV (thermostatic), electronic or capillary tube. TEV may need resizing or replacement for the new refrigerant.
Filter drier size and connectionsWill almost always need replacing during conversion. Note the connection sizes (ODF, flare or solder).
Condenser typeAir-cooled, water-cooled or seawater-cooled. Affects heat rejection and suitability for higher-pressure refrigerants.
Evaporator type and applicationProvision plant, cargo hold, chiller barrel or direct expansion. Each has different requirements for the replacement gas.
Control system / thermostatAnalogue pressostats or electronic controllers? Electronic systems may need recalibration or replacement for new refrigerant pressure ranges.
Sight glass and moisture indicatorNote current colour — moisture present? Needs replacing with desiccant rated for new refrigerant.

4. Current System Condition

Provide an honest assessment of the system's condition. A conversion performed on a system with pre-existing faults will not resolve those faults and may introduce new ones.

Known leaks or refrigerant lossHas refrigerant been topped up repeatedly? Where is the suspected leak? Conversion cannot mask a leaking system.
Current suction and discharge pressuresRecord at normal operating conditions. Helps engineers predict performance change with the new refrigerant.
Suction and discharge temperatures (°C)Superheat and subcooling values help assess TEV setting and whether the valve is correctly sized.
Compressor discharge temperatureHigh discharge temperatures can indicate oil breakdown, wrong refrigerant charge or poor TEV operation.
Any history of contaminationMoisture, air or non-condensables in the system? Acid test results if available. This affects conversion approach.
Recent service historyWhat has been replaced or repaired in the last 2 years? This prevents duplication and helps scope the conversion correctly.

5. Vessel and Operational Information

Vessel name and IMO numberRequired for F-Gas documentation and class notification.
Flag state and classification societySome refrigerants require class approval before use. Your flag state may have specific documentation requirements.
Planned drydock or port call dateConversion timing relative to your next drydock determines the work scope and urgency. Earlier notice = better parts availability and pricing.
Vessel current location and next portRequired for engineer mobilisation planning and parts delivery logistics.

6. Photographs — These Are Essential

No quotation can be fully accurate without photographs. Send clear, well-lit images of:

  • Compressor nameplate (both sides if labels are on different faces)
  • Full system overview — compressor, condenser, evaporator in frame
  • Expansion valve — including the bulb and equaliser line if visible
  • Filter drier — showing the connections and any moisture indicator
  • Sight glass and any visible pipework
  • Any visible defects, corrosion, oil staining or frost patterns
  • Control panel or thermostat

F-Gas Compliance Guide for Vessels & Offshore Facilities

The UK F-Gas Regulation 2024 and EU Regulation 2024/573 impose specific legal obligations on operators of vessels and offshore platforms that contain fluorinated greenhouse gas systems. This guide summarises the key obligations for marine operators.

Who is covered?

Any vessel or offshore platform operated by a UK or EU-based entity, or calling at UK or EU ports, that contains refrigeration or HVAC systems using HFCs, PFCs or SF₆ is covered by F-Gas regulations. This includes provision plants, cargo refrigeration, HVAC chillers, split AC systems and compressed gas fire suppression systems.

Key Obligations

Statutory leak checksRequired at intervals determined by CO₂-equivalent charge: annually (5–50t), every 6 months (50–500t), every 3 months (500t+). Must be performed by a certified F-Gas engineer.
Refrigerant logbookAll systems containing 5 tonnes CO₂-eq or more must have a written equipment log recording charges, recoveries, leak check results and service history. Retained for minimum 5 years.
Leak repair obligationDetected leaks must be repaired without undue delay. A mandatory re-check must be performed within 30 days of repair. You cannot top up a known leaking system.
Certified engineers onlyAll refrigerant handling, leak checking and recovery must be performed by an individually certified F-Gas engineer. The certification number must appear on service records.
No venting to atmosphereRelease of fluorinated refrigerant is prohibited. All recovered refrigerant must be sent to an approved reclamation, recycling or destruction facility.
Phase-down planningR404A, R410A and R407C face tightening quota restrictions through 2024–2030. Operators must plan conversions ahead of drydock schedules while supply is still manageable.
ANP Marine carries full UK F-Gas certification. Every service we perform produces a complete documentation package — equipment log updates, leak check certificates, refrigerant recovery records and compliance statements — formatted for class surveyor and flag state inspection.

F-Gas Leak Check Frequency — Quick Reference Table

Leak check frequency under the UK F-Gas Regulation 2024 is determined by the CO₂-equivalent charge of the system. The table below applies to vessels and offshore facilities.

System Charge (CO₂-eq) Standard Frequency With Automatic Leak Detection Marine Example
5 to <50 tonnes Every 12 months Every 24 months Small provision plants, split AC
50 to <500 tonnes Every 6 months Every 12 months Cargo refrigeration, large chillers
500+ tonnes Every 3 months Every 6 months Large FPSO/platform HVAC
Converting to a lower-GWP refrigerant reduces your CO₂-equivalent charge, which can extend the period between mandatory leak checks. For example, converting from R404A (GWP 3,922) to R448A (GWP 1,387) reduces the CO₂-equivalent of a 30 kg system from 117.7 tonnes to 41.6 tonnes — moving it from the 6-month to the 12-month check bracket.
R22 Conversion Guide — Marine Refrigeration

R22 (HCFC-22) has been banned from use in all equipment in the UK and EU since 2015. Any vessel still operating an R22 system is in breach of ozone and F-Gas regulations and faces enforcement risk at PSC inspections. Conversion is mandatory, not optional.

Recommended R22 Replacement Refrigerants

R407F (GWP 1,825)Operates at similar pressures to R22. Good transitional option where system design limits other choices. Still subject to F-Gas phase-down — not a long-term solution.
R448A (GWP 1,387)Lower GWP HFO blend. Preferred long-term alternative for most marine provision plant and cargo refrigeration applications. Requires POE oil change.
R449A (GWP 1,397)Similar performance to R448A. Suitable for low and medium temperature marine applications. Requires POE oil change and filter drier replacement.
R32 (GWP 675)Very low GWP. Suitable for some HVAC applications but higher pressures require assessment of system design limits before use.

What a Conversion Involves

  • Refrigerant recovery — R22 must be recovered by a certified F-Gas engineer using certified recovery equipment. It cannot be vented.
  • Oil flush and change — R22 systems typically use mineral or alkylbenzene oil. Most HFO alternatives require POE oil. The system must be flushed before recharging.
  • Filter drier replacement — mandatory. The existing drier will be saturated with R22 and mineral oil residue.
  • Expansion valve assessment — TEV may need resizing or replacement depending on the new refrigerant's thermodynamic properties.
  • System leak test — full pressure test with nitrogen before recharging.
  • Recharge and commissioning — new refrigerant charged to OEM specification for the replacement gas, not the original R22 charge weight.
  • Documentation — updated F-Gas equipment log, refrigerant record, service certificate and class notification where required.
