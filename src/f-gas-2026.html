Technical Briefing · Refrigerant Compliance

F-Gas 2026: what the phase-down means for your fleet

A January 2026 milestone tightens the rules on the refrigerants in your vessels' AC and refrigeration plant. Here is what changes, what stays legal, and how the EU and Great Britain regimes differ for a mixed fleet.

For: Superintendents & technical managers Read: 6 minutes Updated: June 2026

The short version

  • From 1 January 2026, the EU bans virgin F-gas with a GWP of 2,500 or more for servicing air-conditioning and heat-pump equipment. The same ban already hit refrigeration plant on 1 January 2025.
  • R-404A and R-507A are the main casualties — their GWP sits far above the threshold. Your plant can keep running, but you can no longer top it up with newly produced gas.
  • Reclaimed and recycled gas is still legal for servicing existing systems — in the EU until 2032, in Great Britain until 2030.
  • Flag and trading area decide which rules bite. EU-flagged vessels and EU port calls follow the EU regulation; UK operations follow the GB retained regime. Most fleets need to track both.

If you manage refrigeration and HVAC across a fleet, "F-Gas" is no longer a once-a-year compliance line. The phase-down of high global-warming-potential (GWP) refrigerants has reached the point where it changes how — and whether — you can service the plant already installed on your vessels. The headline date is January 2026, but the practical squeeze is the steady fall in supply and the steep rise in price of the legacy gases your older plant depends on.

What actually changed

The GWP 2,500 service ban, in plain terms

The mechanism is a "service ban". It does not make your equipment illegal and it does not force you to switch off a working plant. What it prohibits is using virgin (newly manufactured) refrigerant above a GWP threshold to charge or top up that equipment. Once a system leaks down or needs a recharge, virgin high-GWP gas is off the table.

Under the revised EU F-Gas Regulation, the GWP 2,500 service ban applied to refrigeration equipment from 1 January 2025, and extends to air-conditioning and heat-pump equipment from 1 January 2026. That second date is the "F-Gas 2026" milestone superintendents are searching for. Reclaimed or recycled refrigerant of the same type can still be used to keep existing systems running, which is the relief valve most operators will lean on in the short term.

Which refrigerants this hits

The threshold is a hard line at GWP 2,500. The table below shows where the common marine refrigerants fall and what that means in practice. Treat it as a working reference, not a legal determination — your obligations depend on system charge, flag and trading area.

The fleet-management nuance

EU vs Great Britain: not the same rulebook

This is where most generic guidance falls down. Since leaving the EU, Great Britain has run its own retained F-Gas regime, enforced by the Environment Agency, and it does not automatically follow the EU's 2024 revision. For a mixed fleet — EU-flagged vessels, UK-flagged vessels, and ships calling at both — you are managing two timelines at once.

European Union

Refrigeration: virgin GWP ≥ 2,500 banned for service since 1 Jan 2025.

AC & heat pumps: same ban from 1 Jan 2026.

Reclaimed/recycled: permitted for existing systems until 2032; threshold then falls toward GWP 750.

Applies to: EU member states and EU-flagged vessels; relevant on EU port calls.

Great Britain

Service ban: virgin GWP > 2,500 banned for systems of 40 tonnes CO₂e or more since 2020.

Reclaimed/recycled: permitted for existing equipment until 2030 (per REFCOM guidance).

Next phase: DEFRA is consulting on a tighter regime, with new legislation anticipated around 2027.

Applies to: GB operations and UK-flagged vessels.

The takeaway: a vessel that is compliant under one regime is not automatically compliant under the other, and the dates that matter to your ship depend on its flag and where it trades. If you operate across both, plan to the earliest applicable deadline.

What to do about it

Four moves for the superintendent's desk

Inventory your refrigerants

Know which gas, what charge, and which vessels for every plant. R-404A and R-507A systems are your priority list.

Plan retrofits before forced ones

A scheduled retrofit during a yard period costs far less than an emergency one when a system leaks down mid-charter.

Secure reclaimed-gas supply

If you are keeping plant on R-404A for now, line up a certified reclaimed-gas source — supply tightens and prices climb as the phase-down bites.

Keep records inspection-ready

Leak checks, repairs, charges and recovery must be logged and available. A clean F-Gas log is your defence at survey.

Your standing obligations

What stays the same — and mandatory

  • Carry out leak checks at the frequency set by the system's CO₂-equivalent charge, and repair any leak without delay.
  • Recover refrigerant for reclamation, recycling or destruction at service and end of life — venting to atmosphere is prohibited.
  • Keep records of refrigerant type and quantity, maintenance, leak checks and recovery, ready for inspection.
  • Use appropriately certified personnel for any work on F-gas systems.

The direction of travel is clear and it only runs one way: legacy high-GWP gases get scarcer and dearer, and the question stops being "if" and becomes "when, and on whose timetable". Plan the switch on yours.

This briefing is general guidance for fleet planning, not legal advice. F-Gas obligations vary with system charge, equipment type, flag state and trading area, and the rules are under active review in both the EU and Great Britain.
